Knee Arthroscopy

Meniscus Tear

Each knee has two menisci, one on the inner side and one on the outer side. They are crescent-shaped cartilage cushions that spread the load between the thighbone and the shinbone and help keep the joint stable. Protecting them means protecting the knee cartilage in the long run.

Dr. Alberto Sadun Rodríguez is an orthopedic surgeon with a special focus on knee arthroscopy and Head of the Orthopedic Surgery Department at UEESCLINIC Teaching Hospital in Samborondón. He trained at Hadassah Hebrew University Hospital in Jerusalem and keeps up his knee arthroscopy training through Smith+Nephew labs in Andover, Massachusetts.

Two kinds of tears

Traumatic tears happen in young, active people, from twisting with the foot planted or squatting under load. They often come with an ACL tear.

Degenerative tears appear with age, usually after forty, as the tissue wears. They can happen with a minor movement, like standing up from a squat, and often come with some arthritis.

Symptoms

Pain along the inner or outer edge of the knee, especially when twisting or squatting, swelling that shows up within hours or the next day, and clicking. Some tears cause locking: the knee catches and will not fully straighten. True locking needs a prompt evaluation.

When to seek care

Book a visit if pain and swelling do not improve in one or two weeks, if the knee catches or if it gives way. Get care right away if you cannot put weight on the leg, the knee is locked and will not straighten, or you develop calf pain and swelling or shortness of breath.

Diagnosis

The physical exam finds where it hurts and looks for signs of a tear. Standing X-rays show whether there is arthritis, which is key to choosing treatment, and an MRI confirms the type and location of the tear.

Treatment

Degenerative tears, especially when arthritis is already present, are treated first without surgery: physical therapy, strengthening exercise, activity changes and pain control. Most improve. Arthroscopy is not recommended to treat knee arthritis itself.

Arthroscopy is recommended when the knee locks, when the tear is traumatic and repairable, or when pain and mechanical symptoms did not improve with well-done nonsurgical care. Whenever the tear and the tissue allow it, we aim for a repair, which stitches and saves the meniscus. When that is not possible, a partial meniscectomy trims only the torn, unstable piece and keeps the rest.

What recovery is like, in general terms

After a partial meniscectomy, most people can put weight on the leg from day one and recovery takes a few weeks. After a repair, weight bearing and knee bending are limited for several weeks so the meniscus can heal, and returning to sports takes a few months. Exact timelines are set for each patient.

Let us know if you have fever, spreading redness, pain out of proportion, swelling that gets worse, calf pain or swelling, or shortness of breath.

Frequently asked questions

Before your appointment

Does every meniscus tear need surgery?

No. Many, especially degenerative tears, improve with physical therapy and activity changes. Arthroscopy is considered when the knee locks, when there is a repairable traumatic tear, or when pain did not improve with nonsurgical care.

What is the difference between repairing and trimming a meniscus?

A repair stitches the tissue and saves the meniscus, when the tear and the tissue allow it. Trimming (partial meniscectomy) removes only the torn, unstable edge. Recovery is different and we explain it at the visit.

Does a meniscus tear cause arthritis?

Losing part of the meniscus puts more load on the cartilage and, over the years, raises the risk of arthritis. That is why we try to save as much meniscus as possible, with a repair when we can.

Can I keep walking with a torn meniscus?

Many people can walk, but with pain or swelling. If the knee catches or gives way, avoid twisting and sports until it is evaluated.
