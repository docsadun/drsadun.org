Knee pain and injuries

Request an appointment on WhatsApp

If your knee hurts, the first step is an assessment: how it started, a physical exam and, when needed, an X-ray or an MRI. Many knee injuries get better without surgery, with relative rest, physical therapy and medication. When surgery is needed, most knee procedures are done by arthroscopy, through small incisions.

What knee arthroscopy is

Knee arthroscopy is surgery done through two or three small incisions. A camera goes in through one of them so the surgeon can see inside the joint, and fine instruments go in through the others to repair or treat the damaged tissue.

In Dr. Alberto Sadun Rodríguez's practice, this technique is used mainly for anterior cruciate ligament, meniscus and cartilage injuries. Because it is minimally invasive, it usually means less pain after surgery and an easier recovery than traditional open surgery, always depending on the type of injury and the plan agreed with each patient.

Not every knee problem needs arthroscopy. The first step is a full assessment: medical history, physical exam and a review of imaging when needed. Only then is it decided whether treatment can be conservative (medication, physical therapy, activity changes) or whether arthroscopic surgery is the best option.

When it is recommended

Knee arthroscopy may be considered, among other situations, when there is:

  • An anterior cruciate ligament tear with instability that limits sports or daily life.
  • A meniscus tear with pain, locking or the knee giving way.
  • Cartilage injuries that, after assessment, can benefit from an arthroscopic procedure.
  • Some sports injuries of the knee where non-surgical treatment has not been enough.

Patients with knee osteoarthritis are also assessed. In those cases the decision is not based on the diagnosis alone: the type of pain, age, activity level and imaging findings are all taken into account. Arthroscopy is not recommended as a treatment for knee osteoarthritis itself. It may be considered in some patients with mild or moderate osteoarthritis and a meniscus tear that did not improve with physical therapy or other non-surgical treatment. For most, the first plan is non-surgical.

If you felt a pop when you got hurt, have significant swelling, cannot put weight on your foot or your knee feels unstable, it is best to be seen soon. An early diagnosis helps keep the injury from getting worse.

Anterior cruciate ligament

The anterior cruciate ligament is one of the central ligaments of the knee and helps control stability when you pivot. It is often injured in sports that involve cutting, jumping or a sudden stop. Many people feel a pop, the knee swells, and later it feels as if it gives way when turning or going down a step.

Not every injury needs surgery. The first step is a clinical assessment and, when appropriate, magnetic resonance imaging. The first plan may be conservative: control of swelling, physical therapy and a change in activity. Reconstruction, usually by arthroscopy, may be considered when there is instability that limits sports or daily life, when there are associated injuries, or when non-surgical treatment has not been enough. Recovery includes progressive physical therapy and a return to sport only once your doctor clears you. There is no single timeline that fits everyone.

Meniscus

The menisci are two specialized cartilage structures that cushion and help stabilize the knee. They can tear during a twist on a planted foot, often in sport, or wear down over time. Typical symptoms are pain along the edge of the knee, swelling, clicking and, in some cases, locking or the feeling that the knee catches.

Not every tear needs arthroscopy. Many, especially those associated with wear or osteoarthritis, improve with physical therapy, activity changes and pain control. As this page already explains, arthroscopy is not recommended as a treatment for knee osteoarthritis itself. Surgery may be considered when there is pain, locking or giving way that did not improve with non-surgical treatment, or when the tear can benefit from a repair or from trimming. A repair is not managed the same way as a trimming: weight bearing, motion and timelines are set individually.

Synovitis and plica

The inside of the knee is lined by a thin membrane, the synovium, which makes the fluid that lubricates the joint. When that membrane becomes inflamed, we call it synovitis. A plica is a fold of the same membrane that almost everyone has and that, in some people, thickens, gets irritated and causes pain.

Synovitis is not a single disease but a reaction of the knee. It can follow a blow or a twist, come from overuse in sports, appear with a meniscus tear or a cartilage injury, come with osteoarthritis, or be caused by conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or gout. There are also less common forms, such as pigmented villonodular synovitis, in which the membrane grows abnormally.

It shows up as swelling, a full or tight feeling in the knee, warmth, stiffness and pain when bending it. Sometimes the swelling comes and goes with activity.

The plica that most often causes trouble is the medial plica, a fold that runs along the inner edge of the kneecap. Bending and straightening the knee over and over, as in cycling, running or climbing stairs, or a direct blow, can make it inflamed and thick so that it rubs against the thighbone.

It causes pain at the front and inner side of the knee, a click or snap when bending it, and discomfort after sitting for a long time with the knee bent. It is easily mistaken for a meniscus tear or kneecap pain, which is why a proper evaluation matters.

Most cases improve without surgery: relative rest and activity changes, ice, anti-inflammatory medicines when appropriate, and physical therapy to strengthen the quadriceps and stretch the hamstrings. In some cases an injection is recommended. If the synovitis has an underlying cause, such as a rheumatic disease, treating it is part of the plan.

If the plica still hurts after several months of well-done treatment, it can be removed arthroscopically through small incisions. For synovitis that does not settle, or for forms such as pigmented villonodular synovitis, arthroscopy allows the inflamed membrane to be removed (synovectomy) and samples to be taken for testing.

What recovery is like, in general terms

After knee arthroscopy, patients usually go home the same day or the next day, as the surgical team advises.

Recovery is not the same in every case. It depends on what was repaired (for example, a meniscus repair is handled differently from a meniscus trimming or an anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction). In general terms, the process includes:

  • Pain and swelling control as your doctor directs.
  • Putting weight on the leg and using crutches or a knee brace only if indicated.
  • Progressive physical therapy to recover motion, strength and stability.
  • A gradual return to daily activities and, later, to sports, once your doctor clears you.

Exact timelines are set at the appointment and at the post-surgery follow-up. There is no single timeline that fits everyone. Following the rehab plan and reporting warning signs (fever, spreading redness, pain out of proportion, swelling that gets worse, trouble moving your toes, calf pain or swelling, or shortness of breath) is part of a safe recovery.

Before your appointment

What is knee arthroscopy?

It is surgery done through two or three small incisions, using a camera to see inside the knee and treat the meniscus, ligaments or cartilage. Patients usually go home the same day or the next.

Does every knee injury need arthroscopy?

No. Many injuries improve with relative rest, medication, physical therapy and medical follow-up. Arthroscopy is recommended when the clinical exam and imaging show the procedure can help, or when non-surgical treatment has not been enough.

How long does recovery take?

It depends on the injury and the procedure (for example, meniscus or anterior cruciate ligament). The plan for weight bearing, physical therapy and return to sports is set individually at the appointment and follow-ups.

Does every anterior cruciate ligament tear need surgery?

No. Some people recover a stable knee with physical therapy and a change in activity. Reconstruction is considered when there is instability that limits sports or daily life, or when non-surgical treatment has not been enough.

Can I return to sport after this injury?

Return is gradual and is authorized by your doctor, whether treatment was conservative or a reconstruction was done. There is no single timeline for everyone.

Does every meniscus tear need surgery?

No. Many improve with physical therapy and a change in activity, especially when there is wear or osteoarthritis. Arthroscopy is considered when there is pain, locking or giving way that did not improve with non-surgical treatment, or when there is true locking or a repairable tear.

What is the difference between repairing and trimming a meniscus?

In a repair the tissue is sutured, when the type of tear and the quality of the meniscus allow it. In a trimming the unstable edge is removed. Recovery is not the same and is explained at the appointment.
