Knee Arthroscopy

What knee arthroscopy is

Knee arthroscopy is surgery done through two or three small incisions. A camera goes in through one of them so the surgeon can see inside the joint, and fine instruments go in through the others to repair or treat the damaged tissue.

In Dr. Alberto Sadun Rodríguez's practice, this technique is used mainly for anterior cruciate ligament, meniscus and cartilage injuries. Because it is minimally invasive, it usually means less pain after surgery and an easier recovery than traditional open surgery, always depending on the type of injury and the plan agreed with each patient.

Not every knee problem needs arthroscopy. The first step is a full assessment: medical history, physical exam and a review of imaging when needed. Only then is it decided whether treatment can be conservative (medication, physical therapy, activity changes) or whether arthroscopic surgery is the best option.

When it is recommended

Knee arthroscopy may be considered, among other situations, when there is:

  • An anterior cruciate ligament tear with instability that limits sports or daily life.
  • A meniscus tear with pain, locking or the knee giving way.
  • Cartilage injuries that, after assessment, can benefit from an arthroscopic procedure.
  • Some sports injuries of the knee where non-surgical treatment has not been enough.

Patients with knee osteoarthritis are also assessed. In those cases the decision is not based on the diagnosis alone: the type of pain, age, activity level and imaging findings are all taken into account. Arthroscopy is not recommended as a treatment for knee osteoarthritis itself. It may be considered in some patients with mild or moderate osteoarthritis and a meniscus tear that did not improve with physical therapy or other non-surgical treatment. For most, the first plan is non-surgical.

If you felt a pop when you got hurt, have significant swelling, cannot put weight on your foot or your knee feels unstable, it is best to be seen soon. An early diagnosis helps keep the injury from getting worse.

What recovery is like, in general terms

After knee arthroscopy, patients usually go home the same day or the next day, as the surgical team advises.

Recovery is not the same in every case. It depends on what was repaired (for example, a meniscus repair is handled differently from a meniscus trimming or an anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction). In general terms, the process includes:

  • Pain and swelling control as your doctor directs.
  • Putting weight on the leg and using crutches or a knee brace only if indicated.
  • Progressive physical therapy to recover motion, strength and stability.
  • A gradual return to daily activities and, later, to sports, once your doctor clears you.

Exact timelines are set at the appointment and at the post-surgery follow-up. There is no single timeline that fits everyone. Following the rehab plan and reporting warning signs (fever, spreading redness, pain out of proportion, swelling that gets worse, trouble moving your toes, calf pain or swelling, or shortness of breath) is part of a safe recovery.

Frequently asked questions

Before your appointment

What is knee arthroscopy?

It is surgery done through two or three small incisions, using a camera to see inside the knee and treat the meniscus, ligaments or cartilage. Patients usually go home the same day or the next.

Does every knee injury need arthroscopy?

No. Many injuries improve with relative rest, medication, physical therapy and medical follow-up. Arthroscopy is recommended when the clinical exam and imaging show the procedure can help, or when non-surgical treatment has not been enough.

How long does recovery take?

It depends on the injury and the procedure (for example, meniscus or anterior cruciate ligament). The plan for weight bearing, physical therapy and return to sports is set individually at the appointment and follow-ups.
